The Vinland Map

Sixty years of spectroscopy on a map that claimed to show America before Columbus

Download the study (PDF)

What the study is about

In 1965 Yale published a parchment map showing a landmass west of Greenland, labelled Vinlanda Insula. If genuine, it was the earliest known European depiction of America — drawn decades before Columbus sailed.

It was not genuine. What makes the case worth studying is not the forgery itself but how it was exposed: not by historians arguing over handwriting, but by physicists and chemists reading the ink. My study looks at the role Raman spectroscopy played in that verdict.

The decisive evidence

  • TiO₂anatase in the ink — a pigment industrially produced only from the 1920s
  • 1423–1445radiocarbon date of the parchment: genuinely medieval
  • ~60years between publication and the final verdict

The parchment is real. The ink is not. A forger who obtains genuine medieval parchment defeats radiocarbon dating entirely — which is precisely why dating the support and dating the writing are two different problems, and why spectroscopy of the pigment turned out to be the decisive measurement.

Raman spectroscopy identifies anatase unambiguously: it has a strong, narrow band at 143 cm⁻¹ that no medieval pigment produces. It is also non-destructive — you can point a laser at a priceless manuscript and take nothing away from it. For cultural heritage that is not a convenience, it is a precondition.

Why it matters

A single measurement rarely settles anything. McCrone found anatase in 1972; a different team using a different technique found almost none in the 1980s; the argument ran for another thirty years. What eventually closed it was not one decisive experiment but the convergence of several independent methods on the same answer.

That is the part worth carrying over into any analytical work: a result that no one can reproduce, or that one technique alone supports, is a hypothesis rather than a conclusion.
